Transcript

0:00 One job, two skills. This robot can put a bowl on a cabinet. It can open a drawer. Ask for both in one job, and it gets it right in just 1 of 20 fresh tries.

0:14 Skills are not jobs. Robot AI models are learning skills fast. But a business needs whole jobs done reliably, in its own setting, and its jobs keep changing. Turning skills into reliable work is engineering: watch the failures, find the cause, change the system, prove it. Today, specialists do that by hand, for every change, and that makes automation slow and expensive.

0:38 The question. Mayfly is building robot installations whose engineering is done by AI agents. Before building that on real robots, we tested the core question in simulation: can an agent find out on its own what needs to change, change it, and prove that it works? We ran two experiments, one for each kind of engineering an installation keeps needing. The first: a new job, made from skills the robot already has. Every new product or packing rule asks for a job like this, and today a specialist has to make it reliable. The second: faults we planted in a fleet of robots. When robots that worked yesterday start to fail, someone has to find out why, and repair it.

1:22 The test bench. The test bench is a physics simulator with a kitchen scene from a public robotics benchmark. In simulation, every start can be repeated exactly and every motion measured, and the kitchen makes the failures easy to follow. The robot runs X-VLA, an open robot vision-language-action model: it turns camera images and a written instruction into motion. For these experiments we kept it fixed, so every gain comes from the engineering around it.

1:51 The engineer and its sandbox. The engineer is an AI agent in a sandbox. Like a robotics engineer at a desk, it looks at camera frames from its trials and reads the measurements the simulator records: where the hand is, where each object is, what touches what. It writes its own checks, builds programs from the robot's skills and runs experiments. It knows the rules it is graded by, and cannot change them. The faults we planted stay hidden from it, and the exam layouts stay locked away until its solution is final. We built the bench, wrote the requests and fixed the grading. After that, no person took part: the agent chose every experiment and wrote every solution.

2:34 A fair test. Each try starts from a stored arrangement of the table: a layout. The agent compares its programs on the same practice layouts. 20 more stay locked away for a final exam it never sees in advance. On those same 20, we also run the plain chain, the skills simply one after the other. That is the baseline. Later, on real robots, the practice layouts will be simulated copies of the real station, and the final exam will take place in the real world.

3:03 Experiment one: the request. Experiment one. The request, as a shift lead would write it: bowl on top of the cabinet, middle drawer open, everything at rest, hand clear. The bar: the whole job completed without a failure on at least 18 of 20 fresh layouts.

3:22 Watch it fail. Chained plainly, the job falls apart. The drawer skill starts the moment the bowl counts as placed. But the hand still holds the bowl, and drags it off.

3:33 Measure why. The agent studies its first runs and writes a check for exactly this hand-off. In all 10, the next skill began while the bowl was still touched and moving. The placing skill reports success before the job is done.

3:48 One change at a time. Then it changes one thing at a time, on the same practice layouts. Open the fingers first? They still catch the bowl. Go home first? The way home snags it. What works: let the placement finish, so the hand lets go and lifts, then go home, then open the drawer: 10 of 10 successful jobs.

4:10 The final exam. Before the exam, it confirms the program on 20 practice layouts it had never used. 20 of 20. Then the final exam, on the 20 locked layouts. The plain chain gets 1 right. The agent's job: 20 of 20.

4:33 Kept for the next request. The job and its check go into the installation's library, ready for the next request. From request to proven job, the agent took 41 minutes.

4:43 A harder job. The second request is harder. Two things must end up in place: the plate at the front of the stove, and the bowl on top of the cabinet. The robot has a skill for each: one pushes the plate, the other places the bowl. Chained plainly, the job succeeds on 5 of 20 fresh layouts. The trouble is a pack of cream cheese in the plate's way: the push runs into it, or the hand grabs the cheese instead.

5:11 The bottleneck moves. Starting from the check it saved in the first job, the agent tries 18 programs. Loading the cheese into the bowl clears the way, and every plate push works. But now the loaded bowl won't carry: the job delivers 4 of 10. Fixing one problem created the next.

5:30 Set the cheese aside. Its answer: pick up the cheese and set it aside. Then the plate, then the empty bowl: 10 of 10. On the final exam, 20 of 20, against 5 for the plain chain.

5:44 What experiment one showed. What did the first experiment show? Twice, a new job failed when the robot's skills were simply chained. Twice, the agent found the physical cause, changed how the job is done, and proved its solution on layouts it had never seen, with no person in the loop. That matters, because this is the engineering that makes every new job slow and expensive today. Here, an agent did it, in 41 and 113 minutes.

6:12 Experiment two: planted faults. Experiment two asks a different question: when working robots start to fail, can the agent find out why? We built a fleet of 12 simulated arms at 3 sites, doing pick-and-place work, and planted problems in it. The agent is told nothing about them. It sees what an operator sees: results, telemetry, change and maintenance logs, and the actions an operator can take.

6:36 What we planted. Here is what we planted. At the first site, the model that drives the robots runs in the customer's distant data centre, and the line adds a delay. The customer requires that link, so it stays. At the second site, one robot's controller is overloaded and falls behind. At the third site, a software update crops part of every camera image before the model sees it. On the same day, a camera's calibration record is rewritten, which changes nothing at all. That one is our false lead.

7:08 What the agent sees. The agent is not told what causes the failures. It sees the operating records. The robots at the first site trail their commands and succeed far less often. One robot at the second site trails just the same. The robots at the third site fail more often than the healthy robots, and the change log shows two changes on the same day. Same symptom, same cause? And which of the two changes is to blame?

7:33 The agent builds an instrument. The task records can't tell the first two apart. So the agent builds an instrument: a small test motion, with the robot's model switched off, timing how each arm follows a command. At the first site, the hand always starts late, by the same amount: a delay on the line. The robot at the second site starts nearly on time, then falls further and further behind: its controller can't keep up. They look alike, but they are two different problems, and they need different repairs.

8:05 Tested repairs. Each repair is tested on its own before it is adopted. The first site's link has to stay, so the agent has the robots skip commands that would be out of date by the time they arrive. It tests three compensation settings and keeps the best. The overloaded controller at the second site is serviced. At the third site, it undoes each of the two changes in a separate test. Rolling back the software update brings the site back. Rolling back the calibration shows no clear effect. So it adopts the software rollback, and leaves the calibration alone.

8:39 The fleet result. Then the test that counts: 576 attempts the agent never saw. As the fleet was, 451 successes. With the agent's 3 changes, 558. From 78% to 97%. Against what we planted: all three problems found, each with a tested repair, and the false lead left alone.

9:04 The results. In both experiments, the agent did the engineering itself. Two new jobs: from 1 to 20 of 20, and from 5 to 20. A fleet: from 78% to 97%. The robots' vision-language-action model and their overall design stayed the same throughout.

9:24 What we learned. What we learned: the agent worked like a good engineer. It looked at the evidence, found the physical cause, changed one thing at a time, built a measurement when the records weren't enough, and proved its solutions on cases it had never seen. These were first, compact tests, and in each one the agent carried the investigation through to a working result.

9:47 Next: real robots. Next, we take this to real robots: a complete packing operation on two-armed mobile robots, where agents engineer new work on the hardware and prove it there. The question we will answer: how much less human work each new job needs, once agents do the engineering.

10:07 What it means. That is what Mayfly is building: robot installations where taking on new work is part of the product, because agents do the engineering.
