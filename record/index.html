The record behind the film

This page is the record behind the film The installation takes on a new job: what was run, what was counted and which recorded run each number and clip comes from. The tables at the end list every record by an identifier and a SHA-256 checksum, a fingerprint that changes if a single byte of the file changes. Everything happens in simulation, in LIBERO, a public benchmark of household manipulation tasks, on the MuJoCo physics engine.

The installation and its fixed policy

An installation here is one simulated robot arm at a kitchen table, with its objects, the software that runs it and the records it keeps. The arm is driven by a policy: a trained model, X-VLA, that turns camera images and a written instruction into motion. The policy is fixed; none of its weights change anywhere in this film. What changes is how the installation uses it.

A skill is one task the policy does on its own under its own instruction, such as putting an item into a bowl. This installation has 10 skills, the tasks of LIBERO's goal suite, all in one scene. The film opens with 2 recorded runs of single skills; by the delivery check described below, 2 of them delivered.

Story A: the request

The shift lead asks the installation for work and approves its conditions. Here the lead is scripted: its words and its rule for approving were fixed before any run. The request, in the lead's words: "Make this one job: the cheese goes into the black bowl and the bowl goes up onto the cabinet top. The cheese goes in before the bowl moves. The next shift needs it to deliver on at least two of every three starts it is released on."

The requested goals, in LIBERO's spelling, are On cream_cheese_1 akita_black_bowl_1; On akita_black_bowl_1 wooden_cabinet_1_top_side. The order rule is that goal 1 (On akita_black_bowl_1 wooden_cabinet_1_top_side) may not first hold before goal 0 (On cream_cheese_1 akita_black_bowl_1) first holds. It is checked on the recorded trace of every run, and a program whose runs broke it cannot be released. A start is one fresh beginning of the scene, one of LIBERO's stored arrangements of the objects. The installation keeps development starts, on which the agent may try anything, apart from release starts, reserved for one qualifying run at the end and refused to the agent until then.

Delivered is the installation's own delivery check at the end of every run: every requested goal holds, as LIBERO's predicate functions judge it; no part of the robot touches the objects the goals name; those objects are at rest, slower than one centimetre and 0.15 radians a second; and the gripper is open.

Before any program ran, the agent proposed the acceptance condition: run on N = 20 release starts and deliver on at least K = 14 of them. The lead's fixed rule approves any N of at least 20 with K of at least 2/3 of N, and it replied: "Approved: the job is released when it delivers on at least 14 of 20 reserved release starts it was never run on. This condition stands for the whole request."

The request the agent reads changed twice, and the lead's approval rule did not. The host stopped the first attempt and added the sentence "The next shift needs it to deliver on at least two of every three starts it is released on."; until then the agent was not told the share it had to reach, although the lead's rule was already 2/3. After the two completed investigations below, the lead added: "When a program reaches that share on the development starts you tried it on, save it: the reserved starts decide, and a release that misses there is acceptable to me."

Story A: the naive chain

The naive chain is the obvious way to chain skills, run by the host (the people and code that run the installation, outside the agent's reach) as the comparison at the release. It switches to the next skill's instruction the moment LIBERO's native success signal fires, the signal every published LIBERO score counts, and does nothing in between. The first shift's agent began with that chain itself: on development starts 0 to 9 it delivered on 0 of 10. Its account: "This simply switched from the cheese instruction to the bowl instruction when each goal first held. The bowl step timed out on 9 starts."

Story A: the investigation

A job is several skills done in one continuous scene. The agent writes it as a program: up to eight steps, each a policy step with an instruction and a condition that ends it, a short scripted motion of the hand or gripper, a wait, or a return of the hand to where it started. A program is named by a digest of its exact text.

The agent is an AI model, gpt-6-astra at reasoning effort xhigh. A shift is one investigation, in several sessions; between them the installation runs the queued programs and records every control step. A check is a database query over those records, saved with its purpose so that anyone can run it again.

Story A: a second job

A second request asked for another job, with the goals On cream_cheese_1 akita_black_bowl_1; On akita_black_bowl_1 plate_1, and its shift started with job one's library mounted (3 checks). On the same 10 development starts it ran 12 programs in 120 program executions: its first, the obvious chain, delivered on 4 of 10, its best on 6 of 10. It saved nothing: "No program met your development save trigger." It took 5 sessions, 323 tool calls and 47.9 minutes of model time, and left 1 new check and 2 learned skills.

Story A: what changed

After the three shifts, the installation's job library holds 1 saved job, not released, and 4 checks, every one written by an agent; the second shift added 2 learned skills. The robot's policy is the same.

Story B: the fleet

A fleet here is 12 simulated arms at 3 sites doing ordinary pick-and-place tasks from LIBERO day after day, with the same fixed policy. The agent sees what an operator would see: each attempt's outcome, the robots' telemetry, a change log, maintenance and costs.

A lasting condition lowers success and cannot be removed by any action an operator or the agent can take. The public note of site s1 says: "The robots at this site are driven by policy servers in the customer's own data centre, over a wide-area network link. This is the customer's requirement and stays." Commands from a distant server reach those arms late. In regular work on days 0 to 5, before the agent's first decision, that site succeeded on 174 of 288 (60%; 95% interval 55% to 66%) attempts, the other sites on 512 of 576 (89%; 95% interval 86% to 91%).

Story B: the lag that misleads

Ordinary telemetry gives one rough number per robot: how many control steps (50 milliseconds each) its hand trails its commands. A slow command path, a controller that falls behind and a policy that reacts late all raise it. The agent wrote the lesson into the skill it kept: "Task alignment lag alone mixes policy behavior with transport and controller dynamics."

Story B: the probe

A step-response probe is a short scripted motion with the policy switched off: hold still, step the hand along one axis, step back, hold, repeated, while the installation records where the hand is. The response shapes it can show, in the agent's starting skill: a fixed dead time (the hand starts late by the same amount every time), a dead time that grows as a backlog builds, a prompt start that falls short as in a weak arm, and a prompt healthy response, which places the cause elsewhere, such as the cameras.

In round one the agent composed probe p0001 and ran it on 4 robots, 3 times each. Read from its records, after the step out and the step back: r01 (site s1): first moved after 18 and 18 control steps, half-way after 21 and 21; r06 (site s2): first moved after 4, 12 and 5 then 37, 45 and 39 control steps, half-way after 7, 15 and 8 then 40, 48 and 42; r08 (site s2): first moved after 1 and 1 control steps, half-way after 4 and 4; r10 (site s3): first moved after 1 and 1 control steps, half-way after 4 and 4. The agent's reading: "Thus the isolated robot differed in timing, not unloaded reach; the upstream suspect had a normal motion path."

Story B: the second use

A validated probe recipe is the probe and its analysis saved with a learned skill, the robots as parameters, checked when saved and audited before it runs, with one command, on another fleet. The installation kept 3 learned skills from this engagement (library digest beginning 81c70b2a7a9e3b60); separate-fixed-transport-delay-from-controller-backlog carries the probe.

A later fleet has its own remote-link site, s3. The robots were chosen, and a decision recorded from the readings, by the coordinator of the demo acting as the later fleet's operator, from that fleet's public records of its regular work, dated 2026-10-20 02:35 Pacific. At the linked site, r11 succeeded on 30 of 72 (42%; 95% interval 31% to 53%) and r10 on 34 of 72 (47%; 95% interval 36% to 59%). Outside it, the weakest robot was r06, 51 of 72 (71%; 95% interval 59% to 80%); r05, the reference, succeeded on 72 of 72 (100%; 95% interval 95% to 100%). The run's recorded outcome: done. The hands of r11 and r10 first moved after 18 and 18 and 18 and 18 control steps; r06's after 1 and 1, like the reference's 1 and 1.

The skill's rule: "A normal probe with failing tasks calls for image/change-log investigation." On the first fleet the isolated failing robot showed a delay that grew from step to step, and the answer was a controller service. That answer does not fit here: the later fleet's failing robot moves promptly. The decision's next actions: "Next action for r06: inspect its images and its change log, as the skill's rule says for a normal probe with failing tasks" and "Next action for s3: the same adaptation the first fleet adopted for a fixed transport delay, to be confirmed by a task experiment on s3's robots before it is adopted, as the skill requires." Nothing was adopted or simulated on the later fleet.

How this was recorded

Nothing in the film is staged or re-shot; every clip is a recorded run, listed below. A verified re-render re-executes a run's recorded actions at a higher resolution and is used only if every step's physics state and the final delivery check match the original, which is kept. The cut uses 11 re-renders; 11 matched on all 2957 compared steps.

The agent was never given the answer. It could read the requested goals, the definition of delivered and the order rule, and could not change them. The release starts, the evaluation panel, the host's earlier measurements and the causes the host put into the fleets were withheld from it.

Three other attempts at job one are not shown. The first is the attempt the host stopped to change the request; its record holds 1 session. The second failed after 3 sessions on a false alarm of the launch audit: an ordinary English word in the agent's own database query is also a key of a hidden record. The fourth, between the two shifts shown, failed at launch on the same false alarm in the library it mounted. In story B the loop driver stopped at the freeze of the learned skills on a labelling problem in its launch; the host froze the skills and evaluated both states with the driver's own functions, changing no decision of the agent.

Counts are literal. A count resting on at least 20 runs carries a 95% Wilson interval, a standard range for a proportion; versions run on the same starts are compared start by start as rescued and broken.

These are counts from tens to hundreds of runs, in one simulator.
