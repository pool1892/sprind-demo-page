Can an AI agent engineer a robot?

A robot can put a bowl on a cabinet and open a drawer, yet asked to do both, it drags the bowl back off. Finding out why and fixing it is engineering. In two experiments in simulation, an AI agent did that engineering on its own.

In short

The question. Can an AI agent do a robot's engineering on its own: find out what needs to change, change it, and prove that it works?

The evidence. In two experiments in simulation, it did. The agent made two new jobs work from skills the robot already had: both passed every one of their final tests. And it found all three faults we planted in a fleet of robots, building its own measurement instrument when the records were not enough; its repairs raised the fleet's success rate from 78% to 97%. After setup, no person took part, the robot's model stayed unchanged, and every final test used cases the agent had never seen.

Why it matters. Turning robot skills into dependable work takes engineering; done by specialists, it adds cost and time when products and rules change. Here, agents did that engineering. Mayfly is building robot installations that do it themselves, so that customers can change the work without starting a new integration project. We start with fulfilment providers whose assortments and packing rules keep changing.

Why it matters: skills are not jobs

Robot models are learning skills fast: placing an object, opening a drawer. But a business needs whole jobs done reliably in its own setting, with its own products, equipment and rules, and its jobs keep changing. Turning skills into reliable work is engineering, a loop:

  1. Watch the failures
  2. Find the cause
  3. Change the system
  4. Prove it

Today specialists run that loop by hand, job by job and change after change, which adds cost and time whenever the work changes. Mayfly hands the loop to agents. Before building that on real robots, we tested the core question in simulation: can an agent find out on its own what needs to change, change it, and prove that it works?

How we tested it

A test environment where every failure can be seen and measured, a robot model nobody touched, and an agent that never sees the answers.

The test environment

MuJoCo, a physics simulator, runs kitchen tasks from LIBERO, a public robotics benchmark: every starting layout can be repeated exactly, every motion measured, and the failures are easy to see.

X-VLA, an open vision-language-action model, controls the robot, turning camera images and a written instruction into motion. Its weights stayed fixed throughout, so every gain comes from the engineering around the model.

What the engineering agent could do

Two AI models have different jobs. X-VLA executes the robot's skills. A separate engineering agent inspects the results, writes programs and chooses experiments. It works in a restricted environment: it can investigate and change the system through the tools it is given, but it cannot change the grading or see the final tests.

What it could inspect

  • camera frames from its trials
  • the simulator's measurements of the hand, the objects and what touches what
  • in the fleet experiment: operating results, motion and timing measurements, site notes, and change and maintenance logs

What it could do

  • write checks to analyse the records
  • build programs from the robot's existing skills
  • run experiments and test motions
  • save programs and checks for later use

Fixed rules and hidden information

  • grading rules: known to the agent, and fixed
  • the robot model's weights: fixed
  • the causes of the faults we planted: hidden
  • the final tests: locked away until its work was finished

We built the test environment, wrote the requests and fixed the grading rules. After that, no person took part.

A fair test

Each trial starts from a stored arrangement of the objects on the table, called a layout. For each job, the agent developed and compared its programs on practice layouts; another 20 layouts were reserved for the final test, and the pass mark was fixed before any program ran.

On those same 20 final layouts we ran two programs: the robot's skills run one after another (skills in sequence), as the baseline, and the agent's finished program. The difference is what the agent's engineering changed.

Experiment one: making new jobs work with existing skills

Twice, the robot's skills run in sequence failed the job. Twice, the agent found the physical cause, changed how the job is done, and its program passed every final test.

Job one: put the bowl on the cabinet, then open the drawer

The request: put the bowl on top of the cabinet, open the middle drawer, and finish with the objects at rest and the robot's hand clear. To pass, the program had to complete the whole job in at least 18 of 20 final tests.

Run one after another, the skills fail. The drawer skill starts as soon as the placement skill reports success, but the hand is still holding the bowl, so the next motion drags it off the cabinet. The agent wrote a check for this hand-off: in all 10 first trials, the drawer skill began while the hand was still touching the bowl and the bowl was still moving.

Then it tested ways to finish the placement before opening the drawer, one change at a time. Opening the fingers first: 0 of 10; the open fingers still caught the bowl. Returning the arm to its home position first: 6 of 10; the hand snagged the bowl on the way. The program that worked lets the placement finish, so the hand releases the bowl and lifts away, then returns the arm home before opening the drawer: 10 of 10 in practice, and 20 of 20 on further practice layouts it had never used.

Job two: the plate, the bowl, and the cream cheese in the way

The second job is harder: push the plate to the front of the stove and put the bowl on top of the cabinet, with a pack of cream cheese in the plate's path. Run one after another, the skills completed the job in 5 of 20 final tests.

The agent reused and extended the check from job one, then tried 18 programs. Putting the cream cheese into the bowl cleared the plate's path, but the robot struggled to move the loaded bowl: the whole job succeeded in 4 of 10 practice trials. Fixing one problem had created the next. The program that worked moves the cream cheese aside, pushes the plate into place, then places the empty bowl: 10 of 10 in practice, and 20 of 20 on further practice layouts it had never used.

The final test

Once each program was finished, we ran it on the 20 layouts reserved for that job, which the agent had not seen. Skills in sequence and the agent's program faced the same layouts.

From request to proven job, the agent took 41 minutes for job one and 113 for job two. Each program and its check went into the installation's library for the next request.

Experiment two: finding faults in a fleet

When robots that worked start to fail, can an agent find out why? It did: it told two look-alike problems apart with an instrument it built itself, tested every repair, and raised the fleet's success rate from 78% to 97% on final test attempts it had never seen.

We planted three problems in a fleet of 12 simulated robot arms at 3 sites, all doing pick-and-place work, and added a misleading maintenance entry, to see whether the agent could tell a cause from a coincidence:

  • Delayed commands at site 1. The model that drives the robots runs in the customer's distant data centre, and the network connection delays its commands. The customer requires that connection, so it stays.
  • An overloaded controller at site 2. One robot's controller cannot keep up with its commands.
  • Cropped camera images at site 3. A software update crops the images the robot model sees. The same day, a camera's calibration record is rewritten, which changes nothing: the false lead.

The agent is not told the causes. It sees what an operator sees: operating results, motion and timing measurements, site notes (one says the connection at site 1 must stay), change and maintenance logs, and the actions an operator can take.

The same symptom, different causes?

The robots at site 1 moved later than commanded and succeeded far less often; robot r06 at site 2 showed a similar lag. The operating records could not tell whether the two delays had the same cause. At site 3, success had fallen after two changes on the same day: which one mattered?

The agent builds an instrument

The operating records cannot tell the two lags apart, so the agent built an instrument: a short test motion, sent directly without X-VLA, that measures how long the arm takes to follow a small movement out and back. At site 1 the hand started 0.9 seconds late in both directions: a delay on the connection. Robot r06 started after 0.2 seconds on the way out but fell 1.85 seconds behind on the way back: its controller could not keep up. In ordinary work the two failures looked alike; the new measurement told them apart and pointed to different repairs.

Every repair tested on its own

The agent tested every repair before adopting it, on the same tasks and starting layouts with and without the change.

  • The delayed commands. The connection at site 1 had to stay, so the agent compensated for the delay: the robots skip commands that would be out of date by the time they arrive. It tested 3 settings and kept the best.
  • The overloaded controller. The agent tested the simulated maintenance action for robot r06's controller: with it, the robot completed every test attempt, so the agent adopted it.
  • The two changes on one day. At site 3 the agent undid each change in a separate test. Rolling back the software update restored the site; rolling back the calibration showed no clear effect, so the calibration stayed as it was.

The test that counts: the final fleet test

Then we tested the fleet before and after the agent's 3 repairs on the same 576 final test attempts, which the agent had not seen. Before the repairs, 451 succeeded; after them, 558: from 78% to 97%. Against what we planted: every problem found, each with a tested repair, and the false lead left alone.

What we learned

The agent worked like a good engineer: it read the evidence, found the physical causes, changed one thing at a time, and proved its solutions on final tests it had never seen. When the operating records could not tell two faults apart, it built its own measurement.

It also left tools behind. The second job reused and extended a check from the first, and the two finished programs, their checks and its test motion went into the installation's library. That is what Mayfly builds: the tools that let agents measure failures, run experiments and qualify their changes on an installation, so that every tested job and diagnostic becomes a starting point for the next requirement.

What comes next

Next, we take the approach to real robots: two mobile robots with two arms each, from different makers, running a complete packing operation that turns picked items into correct, protected parcels ready for dispatch.

An operator brings an unfamiliar item or a new packing rule, and the agents develop the change: synchronized video, motion and force records explain the failures, and repeated orders give cheap new attempts. The agents practise on simulated copies of the station and qualify their solutions on the real robots; the safety functions stay outside their reach.

Each version is then frozen and tested on unseen orders in separate autonomous tests, without teleoperation, retraining or manual changes between trials; scans, weight checks and independent parcel audits check the result. The measure: all the human work and time each new requirement needs, at fixed parcel quality, throughput and reliability.

What it means

What stands between a robot's skills and paid work is engineering, which specialists do by hand today. Here an agent did that engineering from start to finish and proved it on cases it had never seen.

Our ambition is the economics that follow: smaller installations that recover their setup costs, shorter runs that carry their changeover costs, and customers who buy the result while Mayfly is responsible for delivering it.

That is what Mayfly is building: robot installations where taking on new work is part of the product, because agents do the engineering.
