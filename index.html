Can an AI agent engineer a robot?

Two experiments in simulation

The question. Can an AI agent do a robot's engineering on its own: find out what needs to change, change it, and prove that it works?

The evidence. In two experiments in simulation, it did, with the robot's model unchanged and no person involved after setup. Two new jobs built from the robot's existing skills completed 20 of 20 and 20 of 20 unseen layouts, against 1 and 5 for the skills simply chained. In a fleet with planted faults, the agent built its own instrument, found all three problems and lifted success on 576 unseen attempts from 78% to 97%.

Why it matters. This engineering makes every new robot job slow and expensive today, and agents can do it. Mayfly is building robot installations that take on new work themselves, starting with packing for fulfilment providers.

Why it matters: skills are not jobs

Robot models are learning skills fast, but a business needs whole jobs done reliably in its own setting, and its jobs keep changing. Turning skills into reliable work is engineering, a loop:

  1. Watch the failures
  2. Find the cause
  3. Change the system
  4. Prove it

Today specialists run that loop by hand, for every job and every change, which makes automation slow and expensive. Mayfly hands the loop to agents. Before building that on real robots, we tested the core question in simulation: can an agent find out on its own what needs to change, change it, and prove that it works?

How we tested it

A bench where every failure can be seen and measured, a robot model nobody touched, and an agent that never sees the answers.

The bench

A physics simulator, MuJoCo, runs a kitchen scene from LIBERO, a public robotics benchmark: every start can be repeated exactly, every motion measured, and the failures are easy to follow. The robot runs X-VLA, an open vision-language-action model that turns camera images and a written instruction into motion. We kept it fixed, so every gain comes from the engineering around it.

The sandbox

Two AI models play different parts. The engineering agent reads the records, writes programs and chooses experiments, in a sandbox; X-VLA, the robot's model, executes the skills, unchanged. The agent works from the evidence; the grading stays out of its reach.

Sees

  • camera frames from its trials
  • the simulator's measurements: where the hand is, where each object is, what touches what
  • in the fleet, an operator's records: results, telemetry, site notes, change and maintenance logs

Does

  • writes its own checks over the records
  • builds programs from the robot's skills
  • runs experiments and test motions
  • saves what works in the installation's library

Fixed boundaries

  • hidden from it: the problems we planted
  • locked away until its work is final: the exam layouts and the reserved attempts
  • fixed: the grading rules, which it knows, and the robot model's weights

We built the bench, wrote the requests and fixed the grading. After that, no person took part.

A fair test

Each try starts from a stored arrangement of the table, a layout. The agent compares its programs on practice layouts; 20 more stay locked away for a final exam, and the bar is set before any program runs. The plain chain, the skills one after the other, runs on the same 20: that is the baseline. Experiment one: a new job from skills the robot already has

Twice, chaining the robot's skills failed the job. Twice, the agent found the physical cause, changed how the job is done, and passed the locked exam: 20 of 20, then 20 of 20.

Job one: the bowl on the cabinet, the drawer open

The request, as a shift lead would write it: bowl on top of the cabinet, middle drawer open, everything at rest, hand clear. The bar: the whole job completed without a failure on at least 18 of 20 fresh layouts.

Experiment two: problems planted in a fleet

When working robots start to fail, can the agent find out why? It told look-alike problems apart with an instrument it built itself, tested every repair, and lifted the fleet from 78% to 97% on attempts it had never seen.

We built a fleet of 12 simulated arms at 3 sites, doing pick-and-place work, and planted problems in it:

  • At site 1, the model that drives the robots runs in the customer's distant data centre, and the line adds a delay. The customer requires that link, so it stays.
  • At site 2, one robot's controller is overloaded and falls behind.
  • At site 3, a software update crops part of every camera image. The same day, a camera's calibration record is rewritten, which changes nothing: our false lead.

The agent is not told the causes. It sees what an operator sees: results, telemetry, the site notes (one says the link at site 1 is the customer's requirement), change and maintenance logs, and the actions an operator can take.

What the agent sees

The robots at site 1 trail their commands and succeed far less often; robot r06 at site 2 trails the same way. The robots at site 3 fall short of the healthy robots, and the change log shows two changes there on one day. Same symptom, same cause? Which change is to blame?

The agent builds an instrument

The task records cannot tell the first two apart, so the agent built an instrument: a small test motion with the robot's model switched off, timing how each arm follows a command. At site 1 the hand starts late by the same amount each way, 0.9 seconds: a delay on the line. Robot r06 starts nearly on time, 0.2 seconds, then falls behind, 1.85 seconds on the way back: its controller cannot keep up. They look alike in the task records but need different repairs.

Every repair tested on its own

Each repair is tested on the same attempts with and without it before it is adopted. The link at site 1 has to stay, so the agent has the robots skip commands that would be out of date by the time they arrive: a latency compensation, tested at 3 settings, the best one kept. The agent selects the catalogue's simulated controller service for r06. At site 3 it undoes each same-day change in a separate test: the software rollback brings the site back, the calibration rollback shows no clear effect, so the calibration stays as it is.

Then 576 attempts the agent never saw. As the fleet was, 451 succeeded; with the agent's 3 changes, 558: from 78% to 97%. Against what we planted: every problem found, each with a tested repair, and the false lead left alone.

What we learned

The agent worked like a good engineer: it read the evidence, found the physical cause, changed one thing at a time, built a measurement when the records were not enough, and kept what worked, the two jobs, their checks and its test motion, in the installation's library.

For the second job, it tested and extended the check it had saved from the first. In the fleet, it built a test motion when the task records could not tell two faults apart.

What comes next

Next, real robots: a complete packing operation on two mobile two-armed robots from different makers, turning picked items into correct, protected parcels ready for dispatch. Agents will engineer with physical measurements and experiments, while the safety functions stay outside their reach, practising on simulated copies of the station and proving each solution on the hardware. The measure: all the human work each new requirement still needs, at a fixed operating standard.

What it means

What stands between a robot's skills and paid work is engineering, done by hand today for every job and every change. Here an agent did that engineering from start to finish and proved it on cases it had never seen.

Our ambition is the economics that follow: smaller installations that recover their setup costs, shorter runs that carry their changeover costs, and customers who buy the result while Mayfly is responsible for delivering it.

That is what Mayfly is building: robot installations where taking on new work is part of the product, because agents do the engineering.
